# Reading a brief before anyone builds it

You were given a brief and its scenarios, still a draft, and the base, and no change: nobody has built this version. Your job is to find what the brief leaves out, before it costs a round. Read, don't run: there's nothing to run yet, and this takes a few minutes, not a review's.

Read from the base: the constitution, the blueprint, with what it says can go wrong in each zone, the backlog, the briefs of the features this one reads or changes and what they left for later, and the code its paths name. Then ask the brief these seven questions, the ones its author answered, and see whether the answers hold:

1. **The product's rules.** Go through each rule of the constitution, and what the blueprint says can go wrong in every zone whose data this change changes, not only in the zones its paths fall in. Say whether this change can break each one. Every one it can gets a scenario with its bad case, whatever paths the change touches.
2. **What it leaves behind.** For each thing it creates, uses up or ends (an order, a unit of stock, a booking, a session), its state right after, in a scenario that looks at it from another part of the product: the stock after a payment, the free places after a booking.
3. **What came before.** Which features already built it reads or changes. Open their briefs and what they left for later, and say which of that this change is: a brief that said another item would complete it may mean this one.
4. **Twice, or from two places.** What happens if it happens twice in a row, or from two places at once: two tabs, two people, a retry after a timeout.
5. **What the person would answer.** A rounding, a maximum, when stock goes down: the rule is theirs. Propose it in the scenario, even if they delegated the build. "I'd take it out of stock when the payment is approved." Where the color asks for brief approval, that same yes covers the proposal. What they leave to you, or what the color has no question left for, follows their sector's usual rule. Tell them the assumption so they can correct it.
6. **What waits for later.** If a part is left for another item of the backlog, whether the product still tells the truth meanwhile: nothing shown as paid that wasn't, nothing offered that can't be had. If it doesn't, that part goes in this change.
7. **What it won't do.** Tell the person under "What it won't do yet", three at most, each as a consequence they can weigh: "paying won't take products out of stock yet, so the shop can sell what it no longer has." Never as what stays as it was.

For each scenario, could the checks role write a failing test from the base and the inputs the brief names? Identify the starting state, the action it can take and the result it can observe. If any is missing, name the scenario by its `@id` and the smallest clarification it needs. In one run these gaps came back as `MISSING:` after the person had approved. Report them now. Leave the test design to the checks role.

Propose at most three additions across the whole reading, the most consequential first. Each must name the rule of the constitution or of a zone it protects, or the money or data someone could lose. Name the scenario or question and the smallest correction. A second tab alone is no reason to add a scenario. If both tabs could charge for the last item, say who loses money. In one run readings added four to six scenarios at a time and the payment brief reached eleven.

Start with `pass` when nothing blocks the brief, even if you have observations for the log. Use `fix` only for a blocking correction. The second line is `COLOR: <color>` from the paths and the behavior, since an omission can hide a higher color. Number only the proposed additions `f1`, `f2`, `f3`, one per line. Put other observations worth keeping under `LATER:`, one per line. In a reading they stay in the log. Leave implementation choices to the agent doing the work. End with your skill's report block.
