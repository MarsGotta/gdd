How it is measured

A method that demands facts from agents can't ask you for an act of faith. If GDD works, it has to show up in the data and against how you worked before, and I ask four things of it. That it takes load off you, with more changes finishing without stopping you and less time spent on each one. That it catches what matters, so you can tell what slips through and what each gate catches. That it pays for what it costs in tokens, waiting and keeping the gates up. And that it holds when the project grows, with the cost per change and the failures that escape staying more or less where they were.

If your load goes down but more slips through, you haven't saved anything: you've moved it. So decide in advance what number would make you simplify, and write it in your blueprint, under What would make us simplify, because if GDD can't prove it's worth it, whatever is left over gets cut.

To know whether you're doing better you need something to compare against. The names the industry already uses work, like the DORA metrics (how long a change takes to ship, how often you ship, how many changes fail and how long it takes to recover from one), split by risk and by size, and with them you can also compare yourself with other ways of working.

But the most reliable comparison is the project against itself, before and after, or against similar tasks assigned at random. You measure that baseline before you start, and write it under Baseline in the blueprint, because otherwise "it's going better now" is just a feeling.

And feelings deceive. In a 2025 METR trial, 16 experienced developers took 19% longer with AI, and still believed they had saved 20%. METR believes the speedup has increased since then, but admits its new data is a very weak signal: between 30% and 50% of respondents avoided submitting some tasks if they had to do them without AI.

Gates cost. Naresh Jain measured an agent's environment with two models. On the high-end one, the cost went up 6.5 times and the result didn't improve. The budget one went from 3 to 10 accepted runs out of 10, and still cost about a third less than the high-end one without that environment.

The sample is small and it doesn't count the effort of building it, but it points to a strong argument: with good gates you can use cheaper models.

Seeing a test fail isn't enough either: as Birgitta Böckeler reminds us, it's worth little if nobody checks why it fails.

Where you see it, with the skills

With the skills you don't have to write it down by hand: the layer leaves it in each change's log, and .gdd/gdd metrics --all --summary sums it up for you (The layer's commands).

What you measureWhere you see it
LoadThe minutes of work of each change, not counting what waited for you, and the questions it asked you and how long you took to answer.
What it catchesWhich check failed at the gates and how often, and which changes got through the first time. What slipped through, and which check should have caught it, stays in the log of each hardening.
CostThe seconds of each check, and the time and tokens of each role, where your agent reports them.
ScaleIn the summary, the minutes of work per color. For the rest, .gdd/gdd metrics --all --format csv --out <folder> leaves the tables in files, among them one row per change with its color.

The same data helps you fine-tune your blueprint: which gate really catches things and which one only costs, which zone is badly marked, which approvals last seconds. Today, when you ask, your agent reads the logs while hardening and proposes what to tighten or what to retire, and an engine will do it on its own. Neither changes anything, because touching the blueprint still needs a person. That data stays in your project: nothing leaves on its own. If you ever share something to improve the engine or the ready-made rules, it will be a file you can read first, with no code or names.

It measures the blueprint, not the people. And no number is a target: when it becomes one, it stops measuring.

This version doesn't bring its own data yet. Today I lean on my experience and on what other teams publish, and GDD's data will arrive in a future version. You do have your project's data, from your first change.

What the layer doesn't give yet is keeping that count out of the agents' reach. That's what the engine is for.
