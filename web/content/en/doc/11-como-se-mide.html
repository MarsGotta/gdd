How it is measured

A method that demands facts from agents can't ask you for an act of faith. If GDD works, it has to show up in the data and against how you worked before, and I ask four things of it. That it takes load off you, with more changes finishing without stopping you and less time spent on each one. That it catches what matters, so you can tell what slips through and what each gate catches. That it pays for what it costs in tokens, waiting and keeping the gates up. And that it holds when the project grows, with the cost per change and the failures that escape staying more or less where they were.

If your load goes down but more slips through, you haven't saved anything: you've moved it. So decide in advance what number would make you simplify, because if GDD can't prove it's worth it, whatever is left over gets cut.

To know whether you're doing better you need something to compare against. The names the industry already uses work, like the DORA metrics, split by risk and by size, and with them you can also compare yourself with other ways of working. But the most reliable comparison is the project against itself, before and after, or against similar tasks assigned at random. You measure that baseline before you start, because otherwise "it's going better now" is just a feeling.

And feelings deceive. In a 2025 METR trial, 16 experienced developers took 19% longer with AI, and still believed they had saved 20%. METR believes the speedup has increased since then, but admits its new data is a very weak signal: between 30% and 50% of respondents avoided submitting some tasks if they had to do them without AI.

Gates cost. Naresh Jain measured an agent's environment with two models. On the high-end one, the cost went up 6.5 times and the result didn't improve. The budget one went from 3 to 10 accepted runs out of 10, and still cost about a third less than the high-end one without that environment.

The sample is small and it doesn't count the effort of building it, but it points to a strong argument: with good gates you can use cheaper models.

Seeing a test fail isn't enough either: as Birgitta Böckeler reminds us, it's worth little if nobody checks why it fails.

The same data helps you fine-tune your blueprint: which gate really catches things and which one only costs, which area is badly marked, which approvals last seconds. An engine's report warns and proposes, but it changes nothing, because touching the blueprint still needs the team. The data also helps improve the engine and the ready-made rules. Nothing leaves on its own: it stays in your project, and if you share anything, it's a file you can read first, with no code or names.

It measures the blueprint, not the people. And no number is a target: when it becomes one, it stops measuring.

This version doesn't bring its own data yet. Today I lean on my experience and on what other teams publish, and GDD's data will arrive in a future version.

You can measure by hand, but someone has to write down the attempts and what each change cost. That's what the engine is for.
