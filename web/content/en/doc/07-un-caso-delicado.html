A delicate case

In the shop you reviewed a delicate change. Now you're going inside one: it touches payment, so there's a delicate zone and, as I recommend, your approval.

Imagine you want to add discount codes to your online store. You have what almost any team has: a repository, CI and an agent. No orchestrators, no special tools. And it's a delicate case on purpose, because it touches payment: if the method feels heavy here, everywhere else it will feel lighter.

Before you start, just once. You make passing CI mandatory to merge, and you mark payments/ and CI itself as delicate with a CODEOWNERS. You give the agent its own credentials, with no access to production. You set this up once, and the full list, with the checks branch, is in Start today.

1. You decide what has to happen. You ask an agent for the scenarios, read them and correct them. With this one and two more, an €80 cart and an expired code, you're set:

2. An agent builds the checks. In a separate session, it turns those scenarios into tests:

You run them and they fail, which is what should happen. You make sure they fail because the discount is missing, not because of a broken import, and you save them on a branch the agent can't modify.

3. Another agent gets through the gate. You open another session and hand over the job: "Make these tests pass. Don't touch them. If you think they're wrong, stop and tell me. You have two rounds of CI." And you go do something else.

4. The gate closes. The agent says "done" and opens the PR. CI says no: the €80 test fails, because the solution it wrote is tailored to the first one. Nobody calls you: the error goes back to the agent and it fixes it. In the second round the tests pass, and CI confirms they're the ones you approved.

5. You look only where it matters. Because the change touches payments/, the PR asks for your approval. You read the change, check that it can be undone and give your approval. If it had been the text on a button, you wouldn't even have known.

6. If something slips through, you harden. A week later, someone stacks two codes and the total comes out negative. That's what slipped through. You don't rewrite the process: you add a scenario, "the total never drops below €0," and its test. If that bug comes back, the gate stops it.

And that's it. You've read a few scenarios, given one approval and added one test, without reviewing every step or installing anything. The rest is gates you add when you need them.
