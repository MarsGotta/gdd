The four moments

You do Prepare at the start of the project, and you come back to it when the blueprint falls short. Brief and Execute go with every change. And Harden only comes when something slips through or repeats.

MomentWhenWhat happens
1. PrepareAt the start of the project, and again when the blueprint falls short: there is something new to classify, or a gate is missing, isn't needed or is badly definedIt has two parts. First the blueprint is made: you decide what it's about, what is delicate, which gates are needed and with which checks, the constitution and what context each gate carries, and you can start from ready-made rules. Then machines build the gates the blueprint has defined, and you try to beat them on purpose. Until they hold, no change that has to go through them starts.
2. BriefOn every change, before it startsYou fix what has to pass and a first estimate of the risk is made, from the base in the blueprint, with your approval if needed. For the checks that ask for them, like the tests that start out red, the cases of this change are written before anyone builds.
3. ExecuteOn every changeThe agent works and gets through the gates, one by one, and if it gets stuck, it stops and asks. At the end the risk is recalculated from what was touched, and if it goes up, the change goes back to Brief and has to pass the checks of the new level.
4. HardenWhen something slips through or repeatsWhat slipped through becomes one more check, and the reviewer already carries it in its exam.

Prepare and Brief look alike, but one builds what applies to the whole project, like the standing checks, and the other what applies only to this change, the cases of its custom checks.

They aren't four meetings or four documents. In the shop, Prepare was deciding the constitution, the gates, their context and the traffic light; Brief, the scenarios and color of each change; Execute, the agent against the gates; and Harden, what we changed when someone saw another person's order.

A change, in six steps

Brief and Execute split into five steps, and Harden adds the sixth. They are the steps of the delicate case, where you see them with an example.

StepMomentWhat happens
1. You decide what has to passBriefA few scenarios and the risk of the change are written down before anyone builds.
2. An agent writes the casesBriefAn agent that won't do the work writes them, and you see them fail for the right reason.
3. Another agent gets through the gateExecuteIt works without touching the checks, and if it gets stuck, it stops and asks.
4. The gate closesExecuteThe checks run out of its reach. If they don't all pass, it doesn't go on.
5. You look only where it mattersExecuteA person approves where the blueprint asks for it, with the risk already recalculated.
6. If something slips through, you hardenHardenWhat slipped through becomes one more check.

How much each step weighs is up to your blueprint: there are changes where the first one is just your request and the fifth doesn't exist. Start today sets up the fourth, which is the first gate. Where the blueprint comes from

Prepare has two parts. The first is the blueprint, which defines the base of what gets built, the way someone designs an escape room before opening it: what is delicate, which gates are needed, with their checks, and what each level asks for. You don't have to start from scratch, because you can start from ready-made rules for your area, which is the idea behind The kits.

The second is building what that blueprint has defined and seeing that the gates close. Until then, no change that has to go through them starts.

When the blueprint falls short

The blueprint doesn't stay still. Some zones evolve by their nature, and as you iterate you keep adding elements to them. You also come back to Prepare when you see that a gate isn't well defined, or that one is missing or isn't needed. Often you see it when you harden: what slipped through calls for a new check, and adding it to the blueprint and building it is Prepare again.

What the blueprint doesn't cover yet never comes out green, because green is only what you have marked as green. It isn't red just like that either: an agent that won't do the work analyzes it and writes down why, and if it isn't sure, it asks you. The change goes on at the level it gave it, and afterwards the agent proposes how to leave it in the blueprint, which only changes with your yes.

What is never decided inside a change is relaxing a check. If mutation testing, which breaks the code on purpose to see whether the tests notice, only pays off in what is delicate, or only on what was touched, the blueprint says so, and each change falls under that rule or it doesn't.

The blueprint doesn't ask anything new of you: it's put together with what you already have in the repository. How, in Start today.
