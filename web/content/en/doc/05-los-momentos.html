The five moments

You do Prepare at the start of the project, and you come back to it when the blueprint falls short. Brief and Execute go with every change. Close comes once the change is in use. And Harden comes when something slips through or repeats, and also when a gate stops adding anything.

MomentWhenWhat happens
1. PrepareAt the start of the project, and again when the blueprint falls short: there is something new to classify, or a gate is missing, isn't needed or is badly definedIt has two parts. First the blueprint is made: you decide what the product is for and what can never happen in it, which is its constitution, what is delicate, which gates are needed and with which checks, what context each gate carries and how work gets closed, and you can start from ready-made rules. Then the gates the blueprint has defined get built. You build them, or your team does, with the agents and tools you use, and you try to beat them on purpose. An engine can make it easier, but it doesn't take you out of it: this is the most delicate part of the method. Until they hold, no change that has to go through them starts.
2. BriefOn every change, before it startsYou start by writing down what has to be true: the rules of what the change touches, a few scenarios and, if it promises something to whoever uses it, what is expected, what signal will tell and where it will be looked at. With that, a first estimate of the risk is made, from the base in the blueprint, with your approval if needed. For the checks that ask for them, like the tests that start out red, the cases of this change are written before anyone builds.
3. ExecuteOn every changeThe agent works and gets through the gates, one by one, and if it gets stuck, it stops and asks. At the end the risk is recalculated from what was touched, and if it goes up, the change goes back to Brief and has to pass the checks of the new level. When it gets through all of them, the change is built, but not closed yet.
4. CloseOnce the change is in useYou look at whether it helped, against what was written when it was briefed. The agent gathers what can be seen and the verdict is yours, or belongs to whoever answers for the product on your team: yes, not yet or no. Until then the change waits in plain sight: it doesn't close by itself, it isn't canceled and it doesn't hold up what comes next.
5. HardenWhen something slips through or repeats, and when a gate stops adding anythingWhat slipped through becomes one more check, and the reviewer already carries it in its exam. What slipped through because nobody had written it down becomes a rule, with what checks it. What no longer adds anything is relaxed or retired, and its rule with it. Both change the blueprint, so you go back to Prepare and you approve them, or your team does.

Prepare and Brief look alike, but one builds what applies to the whole project, like the standing checks, and the other what applies only to this change, the cases of its custom checks.

They aren't five meetings or five documents. In the shop, Prepare was deciding the constitution, the gates, their context and the traffic light; Brief, the scenarios and color of each change; Execute, the agent against the gates; Close, going back to the size filter a few days later and looking at whether people use it; and Harden, what we changed when someone saw another person's order.

A change, in seven steps

Brief and Execute split into five steps, Close adds the sixth and Harden the seventh. They are the steps of the delicate case, where you see them with an example.

StepMomentWhat happens
1. You decide what has to passBriefThe rules the change touches, a few scenarios and its risk are written down before anyone builds.
2. An agent writes the casesBriefAn agent that won't do the work writes them, and you see them fail for the right reason.
3. Another agent gets through the gateExecuteIt works without touching the checks, and if it gets stuck, it stops and asks.
4. The gate closesExecuteThe checks run out of its reach. If they don't all pass, it doesn't go on.
5. You look only where it mattersExecuteA person approves where the blueprint asks for it, with the risk already recalculated.
6. You look at whether it helpedCloseWith the change already in use, you give your verdict against what was written in the first step.
7. If something slips through, you hardenHardenWhat slipped through becomes one more check.

How much each step weighs is up to your blueprint: there are changes where the first one is just your request, the fifth doesn't exist and the sixth settles itself, when its time passes without doing harm. A gate by hand sets up the fourth, which is the first gate. The shop spreads them over the four gates that project needed, and The whole path takes them my way, with five.

What has to be true

Brief starts by writing it down, and it doesn't take a new document: these are short rules and scenarios that a gate can check. They live in three places.

About whatWhat gets writtenWhen
The productWhat it is for and what can never happen in itIn Prepare, with the blueprint
What the product does, like charging, signing in or applying a discountIts rules: what always holds and what must not be possibleIn Brief, the first time a change touches it and every time its rules change
The changeA few scenarios and, if it promises something to whoever uses it, what is expected, what signal will tell and where it will be looked atIn Brief, every time

Each rule says what checks it: a case that runs in the gates, the reviewer, your approval or what gets looked at when closing. One that has nothing to check it isn't deleted: it stays as a question. You approve the rules where your blueprint asks for it, and the builder doesn't touch them, just as it doesn't touch the checks.

Not every change opens a rule. A typo, or a tweak that changes none, stays in its scenarios, and sometimes in your request.

When a change is closed

Getting through the gates leaves the change built. It is closed when someone has looked at whether it helped, and for that it has to have been said beforehand: those are the three lines from Brief.

The agent gathers what can be seen, from where the builder can't fake it, and the verdict is yours, or belongs to whoever answers for the product on your team. There are three: yes, not yet and no. A no closes it too, and whatever has to be done next is a new brief.

There is no deadline. Until someone has looked, the change waits in plain sight, with how long it has been there: it doesn't close by itself, it isn't canceled and it doesn't hold up what comes next. You answer when you come back, as with an approval.

What promises nothing to whoever uses it, like a typo or a dependency update, closes when its time passes without doing harm. Your blueprint says what falls there, and also who closes. And if a request was split into several changes, it is closed once, whole.

Close passes the same four questions as a gate: it was decided beforehand, it doesn't trust what the agent says, it stays out of the agent's reach and it has to be able to say no. A closing that never says no is a drawing on the wall.

Where the blueprint comes from

Prepare has two parts. The first is the blueprint, which defines the base of what gets built, the way someone designs an escape room before opening it: what is delicate, which gates are needed, with their checks, and what each level asks for. You don't have to start from scratch, because you can start from ready-made rules for your area, which is the idea behind The kits. And if the project already has history, before you approve the blueprint you can run its zones over the last commits and see how many would have come out of each color.

The second is building what that blueprint has defined and seeing that the gates close. Until then, no change that has to go through them starts.

When the blueprint falls short

The blueprint doesn't stay still. Some zones evolve by their nature, and as you iterate you keep adding elements to them. You also come back to Prepare when you see that a gate isn't well defined, or that one is missing or isn't needed. Often you see it when you harden: what slipped through calls for a new check, and adding it to the blueprint and building it is Prepare again.

What the blueprint doesn't cover yet never comes out green, because green is only what you have marked as green. It isn't red just like that either: an agent that won't do the work analyzes it and writes down why, and if it isn't sure, it asks you. The change goes on at the level it gave it, and afterwards the agent proposes how to leave it in the blueprint, which only changes with your yes.

What is never decided inside a change is relaxing a check. If mutation testing, which breaks the code on purpose to see whether the tests notice, only pays off in what is delicate, or only on what was touched, the blueprint says so, and each change falls under that rule or it doesn't.

The blueprint doesn't ask anything new of you: it's put together with what you already have in the repository. With your agents, in Get started; by hand, in A gate by hand.
