The seven principles

You can have gates, checks and a blueprint and still get nothing out of them. Each of these principles covers one specific way for that to happen.

1. Gates come first, and nobody skips them, not even you.

If you decide what to check once you can already see the result, you end up checking what the agent did and not what you needed. That's why gates are defined before the work, and if you skip one, however much of a hurry you're in, you're no longer doing GDD. Not even emergencies are an exception: they have a gate of their own, also defined in advance.

2. A gate that never closes is not a gate.

It's a drawing on the wall: it has its frame and everything, and it doesn't stop anybody. Before trusting a gate you have to see that it closes, and that's why a new check has to fail for the right reason and catch wrong solutions. With no code, even an empty test fails, but because of a broken import, and that proves nothing. If a return 40 passes the payment test, the check is hollow. In a refactor, where the tests already pass, you check it by breaking the code on purpose.

Meta takes the idea to large scale: it generates faults on purpose with an LLM, along with tests that catch them.

3. To each gate, its context.

A gate without context is a wall: the agent guesses, fails and guesses again, and you pay for every round. That's why each gate carries what it takes to get through it, and only that. The scenario gate gets the request and a design; the test gate gets the scenarios and the data model. What applies to the whole flow, like the project's constitution or the skills for its technologies, belongs to no gate: it's the cross-cutting context, and the agent always carries it. That and its gate's context, which can include what the previous gate generated, is all it gets, so it starts with a clean context. I hand it out like this, gate by gate, because it's what works best for me, although I haven't measured it yet.

More isn't better. In a study presented at ICLR 2026, repository-wide context files didn't improve the agents' success and raised the cost by more than 20%, and its authors recommend that hand-written ones ask for the bare minimum. OpenAI got there on its own: give an agent "a map, not a 1,000-page instruction manual". That's why the cross-cutting context, which goes along on every call, has to be simple and clear.

And context helps the agent get through, but it proves nothing. However good it is, what opens the gate is still a check, and that's the difference from a long specification: telling it something doesn't make sure it happens.

4. You get through a gate with facts, not words.

Saying "done" opens nothing, because an agent can say it about an empty shell, a mockup that looks like it works and is hollow inside. Without facts it doesn't get through. If it gets stuck, it stops and asks, and attempts, time and cost are counted so it doesn't keep going in circles.

Being able to stop matters: in ImpossibleBench, letting it ask a person for help brought GPT-5's cheating down from 54% to 9%, although it didn't work the same in every model. The cap counts too. At Anthropic, one PR went through more than fifty rounds in a single night, and Stripe cuts off at two rounds of CI.

5. Whoever goes through the gate doesn't decide how it's validated.

The agent never touches the tests that judge it, just as a student doesn't grade their own exam, and it can't be its own reviewer either. A test saved in the repository can be rewritten with no effort to fit the code, and a test that contradicts what was asked is exactly what pushes it to do so. That's why, if it thinks a check is wrong, it stops and says so.

Whoever made the check or a person fixes it, never whoever goes through the gate. Fixing it creates another version, and if it changes what has to pass and your blueprint asks for that approval, it goes back to a person.

Leaving the tests read-only keeps the agent from modifying them, according to ImpossibleBench, but other tricks remain, and that's what reviewers are for.

6. More risk, more checks, and your approval only where it matters.

Three lines in payment weigh more than an entire help screen: the size of the change doesn't decide, what can go wrong does. And more risk doesn't always mean more tests: often it's another type of check, like a reviewer or a person. At Anthropic, the change sets the risk level and each level calls for zero, one or two people, and only trusted authors can use the lightest paths.

You come in where your blueprint says so. The gates handle the rest, because someone who has to approve everything ends up approving without reading. If you want to look at something more, you add it yourself as a person's check; the method doesn't require it.

Addy Osmani calls it a lit factory (the same process, with the lights on where judgment is needed). Turning them all off has a price, comprehension debt: the gap between how much code exists and how much anyone truly understands.

7. Gates with cracks get hardened.

A crack is a weak point in a gate, and you notice it when something slips through: an incident, or a change that had to be undone or fixed after it was considered finished. What slips through, or repeats, becomes a check and a question on the reviewer's exam. A reviewer only counts if it catches what already slipped through or, at the start, faults planted on purpose.

Hardening is also knowing when to reduce. Every failure adds a check, and if you never remove one, each change takes and costs a little more than the last. A gate that no longer catches anything, or that costs more than it protects, is relaxed or retired, on the same evidence you would ask for to add it and after seeing that it still closes (how is in practice 27). The two keep the balance, and that way, over time, the method interrupts you less.

Anthropic does something very similar: each incident becomes an exam case for its automated reviewer. With its review system, in one of its repositories, merged PRs more than doubled from June to September 2026, and the share that had to be reverted fell by more than 40%.
