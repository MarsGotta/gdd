The pieces and the roles

The pieces

You saw the same scene several times in the shop: the agent said "done" and a test said no. Behind that scene are almost all the pieces of the method.

Gate
What the agent has to prove to move on. With the payment gateway, that the purchase did what the scenarios say, even if someone clicks twice. It opens only if all of its checks pass.
Check
What proves the gate is met, like the tests another model had created, among them the one that expects a single €40 charge after two clicks. Nobody asks the agent whether it has finished.
Risk
The level of each change: green, yellow or red. The text of a button is green, because little goes wrong if it fails, and the payment gateway was red, and it would have been even at three lines. You define it when you decide what is delicate, and it's measured by what the change really touches, not by what the agent says about it. What can't be undone is never green.
Blueprint
Where what is delicate, which gates are needed, with their checks, and what each level asks for are written down. In the shop it fell short on orders, and that's why it had to change. Your agent drafts it and it only changes with your yes, or your team's: the builder doesn't touch it, just as it doesn't touch the checks.
Context
What the agent needs to get through each gate. To each gate, its own.
Constitution
The main piece of the context the agent always carries. It starts with five lines about the product, like who it is for and how you'll know it serves them, and goes on with its stack, its commands and what can never happen in it.
Reviewer
Another agent, in its own session and with a clean context. It reads the change against what has to pass and, where the risk calls for it, runs it.
Bounded environment
Where the agent works: with no access to production and with just the credentials it needs. A person confirms anything irreversible.

Who checks

The payment gateway's tests are machine checks, and there are two kinds. The custom ones are in the blueprint like the rest, but their cases are written on each change and set what has to pass, like the one for the two clicks. The standing ones apply to the whole project and watch quality, security and proportion, with a linter or a size cap per change.

And watch out, a check is more than the test: it also includes everything that decides its result, like scripts, configuration, references or a scanner's exceptions.

On top of the machine you can add a reviewer (another agent) or a person who approves, and for the most delicate changes a second person, if your team turns that on. Neither the reviewer nor the people let you skip the minimum the risk calls for. They don't get mixed up with each other either: what a reviewer passes never counts as a person's approval.

You always approve the blueprint, or your team does, and the blueprint says where else an approval is needed, including the approval of what has to pass. And when I say just your team, I mean whoever uses the method: you, if you work on your own.

Context

A gate says what the agent has to prove, but not how to get there. With nothing else to go on, it guesses: it fails, guesses again, and you pay for every round.

What you give it so it can get through is its context: the request, a Figma design or the data model. It can live in the repository or come from outside: an MCP server, an API, a loose document, an image. Each gate gets what it needs for that gate, because handing over everything at once doesn't help either.

Part of it belongs to no gate, because the agent needs it throughout the flow: how the project works, which tools it uses, the skills for its technologies. I call that the cross-cutting context. Its main piece is the constitution, and since it goes along on every call, it's what costs the most, so the simpler and clearer, the better.

That and its gate's context, which can include what the previous gate generated, like the scenarios for creating the tests, is all it gets: every agent starts with a clean context, without anyone else's conversation.

Checks make the gate real, and context makes it possible to get through. Without the first, the gate is a drawing on the wall. Without the second, it's a wall.

That said, context proves nothing: however good it is, what opens the gate is still a check. The agent doesn't rewrite its own instructions or skills, and context never includes what judges the agent, like hidden examples. If an agent keeps failing at the same gate, check whether it's missing context before you harden the gate.

Better if it's deterministic

A gate works better the more deterministic it is: the same change always gets the same result, like a test or a linter. When that isn't possible, you reinforce it in other ways so the agent doesn't leave the lane or slip through a hole.

Other teams already work this way. At Stripe, steps like running the linters don't call any model: they just run code. After the deterministic verifiers, Spotify adds an LLM-based judge that vetoes about a quarter of the sessions.

The gates judge what comes out, not what the agent does while it works, and a destructive command happens before it reaches any gate. That's also why the agent works in a bounded environment, with no access to production and with just the credentials it needs, and why a person confirms anything irreversible.

Where the escape room comparison breaks down

The escape room in The idea breaks in two places. In the game, opening the last door means you won. Here it only means the agent got past what could be checked, which may be less than you needed, as with My orders, which got through all four gates and let people see someone else's orders.

And the agent isn't your rival: there are no puzzles to guess, because it knows in advance almost everything it has to prove. What it can do is take shortcuts even when it knows that's not what you asked for, as METR has observed, and that's why the gate doesn't depend on its goodwill.
