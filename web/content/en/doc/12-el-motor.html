The engine

With permissions, branch protection, CI and an agent that uses its own credentials you can already enforce a good part of the seven principles, but not the whole core. What's left out is whatever needs someone to keep count of the entire change, and that is what an engine does.

What is missingWhy
Limits per changeEach agent has its own cap, but nobody adds up the attempts, time and cost of the whole change.
Nothing is loosened without approvalWhoever administers the repository can remove the branch protection in the settings, without going through any gate.
A complete logGit and CI keep who did what and which gate judged it, but not what it cost; measuring stays manual.
The order of the gatesCI checks at the end, but nothing forces the order before the PR.

Any engine that meets those guarantees will do, including one you write yourself. Mine is T-minus: I want it to enforce the core, bring kits and let you create your own or load other people's, and give you the data to see what to improve. It's still in development and doesn't do all of this yet, so what follows is what its version 1.0 has to do, not what it does today.

The same journey, with no engine and with T-minus

It's what we did in Hands on, first by hand and then the way T-minus will do it.

The method tells you what has to be done, and the engine, how surely it gets done.

When you need an engine

You don't need one on day one. It starts to be necessary when there are several agents or many changes at once, when you have to show what happened or measure whether GDD pays off, or when there's no CI underneath, as is often the case outside software.

What the engine doesn't replace are the good practices, which are a separate thing: how to keep the principles true at each moment and which checks to put at each stop. They're my proposal, not a rule.
