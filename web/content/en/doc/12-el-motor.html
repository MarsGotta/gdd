The engine

With permissions, branch protection, CI and an agent that uses its own credentials you can already enforce a good part of the seven principles, but not the whole core: that is the platform degree, the second of the three.

The skills and their layer already keep count of the whole change, but an agent runs the layer and it's within that agent's reach. What's left out is whatever needs that count to be kept by something no agent can touch, and that is what an engine does.

What is missing without an engine

What is missingWhat you have todayWhy it isn't enough
Limits per changeThe layer adds up the attempts, time and tokens of the whole change, and doesn't let another round start when a limit runs out.It's within reach of the agent that runs it.
Nothing is loosened without approvalIf you apply them on your platform, your protected branches and your CODEOWNERS ask for a person to change the blueprint.Whoever administers the repository can remove the branch protection in the settings, without going through any gate.
A complete logThe layer records who did what, which gate judged it, how long it took and, where your agent reports them, how many tokens it used. Your CI checks that the log is whole and in order.A tool within the agent's reach writes it: CI sees whether it's broken, not whether something was never written down.
The order of the gatesThe layer refuses steps out of order, and your CI runs the gates on the exact commit.The layer can be skipped. CI can't, but it comes at the end, before merging.

Any engine that meets those guarantees will do, including one you write yourself.

When you need an engine

You don't need one on day one. It starts to be necessary in these cases:

  • When there are several agents or many changes at once.
  • When you have to show what happened.
  • When the measure of whether GDD pays off has to be proof no agent could have touched.
  • When there's no CI underneath, as is often the case outside software.

What the engine doesn't replace are the good practices, which are a separate thing: how to keep the principles true at each moment and which checks to put at each stop. They're my proposal, not a rule.

The same journey: by hand, with the skills and with T-minus

The engine I'm building is T-minus. I want it to enforce the core, bring kits and let you create your own or load other people's, and give you the data to see what to improve.

It's still in development and doesn't do all of this yet, so its column is what its version 1.0 has to do, not what it does today.

It's what we did in Hands on: by hand, with today's skills and the way T-minus will do it.

Part of what T-minus will bring is ready-made rules for an area. What they are, and what they'll bring, is in The kits.

Do permissions, protected branches and CI meet the whole core of GDD?

  • Yes: with them no agent can touch the checks or the main branch.
  • No: they cover a good part, but whoever administers the repository can still loosen them.

That's it: that's the platform degree. The whole core needs nobody to be able to remove the protection without a person approving it, and the count to be kept by something out of every agent's reach, and that's what an engine does.
