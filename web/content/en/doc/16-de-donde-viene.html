Where it comes from

GDD doesn't set out to invent anything: it standardizes what has worked for me and what has worked for others. What it adds is a common language, a test to know whether a gate is valid and a path that ties risk to hardening.

Others point in the same direction, although they aren't where GDD comes from. Boundary-First Engineering (2026) argues that whoever implements can't be whoever verifies. And The AI Coding Agent Manifesto, by Shay Cohen at Wix Engineering (2026), calls for not letting the same AI write and judge the code.

Not everyone in the industry thinks the same, either. OpenAI, with a lot of volume, argues for few blocking gates because "corrections are cheap, and waiting is expensive," although it admits that would be irresponsible at low volume. GDD settles it by risk: on Green, a gate can be left open and whatever fails gets fixed afterward; on Red, you wait.
