What each color asks for

A change gets the color of the most delicate thing it touches, and its color decides what it has to prove, who approves it and how much it can spend.

What follows are the values the blueprint's template starts with. Your blueprint decides: you can change any of them, you or your team, and your logs will tell you which ones to change. Removing something loosens a gate, so you approve that too.

The names in your blueprint

What a person approves and what the reviewer does have names among each color's checks, under colors in gdd/blueprint.md. That's where you remove or add them, on any color:

brief-approval
Your approval of what has to pass, before anyone builds. The template asks for it on yellow and red.
review
The reviewer: an agent with a clean context, which hasn't seen how the change was made. Each finding it asks to fix carries its citation.
person-review
Your review of the change at the end, with the evidence the reviewer leaves you. The template asks for it on red.

If you remove brief-approval from yellow, the brief and its scenarios are still written: your agent tells you in one line what the change will do and doesn't wait for you.

For every color

  • Two tiers of checks. Each round, the seal, the tests, lint and secrets, under two minutes together. Then, once before the reviewer, what takes longer, such as a browser test of what the change touches when it touches what a person sees, under ten minutes in all.
  • The reading of the brief. When a reviewer reads the brief before anyone builds, your agent can only drop a finding of that reading by citing the line that answers it.
  • A notice for size. A change of more than 400 lines goes ahead, but the reviewer knows, and it reaches you in one line.
  • When a limit runs out, no new round starts, but what's already built still goes through its gates and its review.
  • What no zone covers is never green. Your agent judges it in each change, yellow unless it does what a red zone does, and asks you only when it can't tell.
  • The gates of each zone. On top of what its color asks for, a change goes through the gates of the zones it touches, when a zone has its own. If the blueprint names a zone's gates file and that file is missing, its gates don't run and the layer says why.
  • Mutation and coverage aren't there at the start. Harden adds them, at their tool's own default, when the tests proved weak. A check that some colors ask for and others don't judges only what the change adds or modifies.
  • Hidden examples are for red to ask for, only from the platform degree up: more examples of the same scenarios, where the builder can't read them.

All of this is written down in files in your repository, and each one is in What it leaves in your repository.

Who decides that on yellow you approve what has to pass?

  • Your blueprint: the template comes that way, and you can change it, you or your team.
  • The method: a yellow change always waits for your approval.
