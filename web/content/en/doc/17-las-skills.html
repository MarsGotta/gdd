The skills

With the skills installed and your blueprint approved, as Get started explains, every change follows the same path. What follows is day to day: what you say at each moment, what reaches you and what you do when something stops.

A skill is a file of instructions your agent loads when it needs it. The GDD ones use what your project already has, its tests, its linter, its CI and git. The only things they bring that run are the layer, a small command that keeps the count of every change, and a couple of scripts they offer you when preparing, which become your project's if you accept them. One is the entry skill, gdd, which hands out the work, and there are seven more, one per moment or role.

The eight

SkillWho loads itWhat it leaves

gdd-briefThe orchestrator, on every change, green tooThe change's color and, on yellow and red, the rules of what changes, their scenarios and the expected risk.
gdd-checksAnother agent, never the one that buildsThe standing checks and the cases of each change, each seen failing for the right reason.
gdd-buildThe agent that goes through the gateThe change, and the facts that prove it.
gdd-reviewThe reviewer, with a clean contextOn red, what the brief leaves out, before anyone builds. Then its verdict, and the risk worked out again.

gdd-hardenThe orchestrator, with you, when something slips through or a gate stops adding anythingThe crack and the check that would have caught it, or the proposal to retire the one you don't need.

Whoever writes the cases, whoever builds the change and whoever reviews it each work in a separate agent, which starts without the others' conversation: it gets what they left written, not how they got there. That way the reviewer judges the change in front of it, without having heard how it was made (The pieces and the roles).

Checking and updating them

The commands to install them on Claude Code, Codex and Antigravity are in Get started. To find out whether they're in, and to update them later on, each agent has its own, which you type in a terminal:

A session that was already open doesn't load the new version: open another one after updating.

If you'd rather keep them inside your project, copy the eight folders whose names start with gdd from the repository into .claude/skills/ on Claude Code or .agents/skills/ on Codex. On Claude Code, also copy the subagents in adapters/claude-code/agents/ into .claude/agents/. Copied by hand, they don't bring the layer: build it from a clone of the repository with sh engine/build.sh, and copy the binary it points you to into .gdd/gdd in your project, with .gdd/ in its .gitignore.

The layer

With the skills comes gdd, a small command I call the layer. It keeps the order of every change, counts its limits and writes its log, so your agent doesn't keep that count from memory. If the agent tries a step out of order, the layer refuses and tells it which one is next.

It comes as source, in the plugin's engine/ folder. The first time your project needs it, your agent builds it and leaves it at .gdd/gdd, which git ignores. Building it takes Rust 1.89 or later: if you don't have it, your agent tells you in one line and waits. The first build downloads what it depends on from crates.io, so it needs the network that once. It works on macOS and Linux; nobody has tried it on Windows yet. Your CI builds the same command, to check every change's log out of the agents' reach.

You don't have to use it yourself, but three commands are worth knowing, from your project's folder:

The first tells you what comes next in your project: what's under way, the next thing on the list and the changes in use that wait for your verdict. The second lists every question waiting for you, oldest first, with how long it has waited. The third says how your changes went: how they ended, how long each took without the waits for you, how often the gates and the reviewer passed them the first time, and what each check costs. These and others, with an example of what they answer, are in The layer's commands.

The layer isn't an engine. While an agent runs it, it's within the agent's reach, and what keeps it honest is your CI.

Prepare, more slowly

Prepare is the first conversation, and it only comes back when the blueprint falls short. The short version is in Get started; this is what goes on in it. It needs your agent's deepest model: if you aren't on it, it tells you before it starts.

  1. In a new project, or if you leave the decisions to it, it reads up before it proposes anything, because tools and a sector's rules change faster than a model's memory. It looks up what a project like yours has to protect, what its sector asks for and which tools are current for what you use, and keeps a copy of each source. In what it proposes, it tells apart what comes from a source and what is its own default.
  2. It proposes the zones of your project and the gates it needs, and you correct what doesn't fit. Each gate is written as a short Gherkin feature: what it asks of each color and what it rejects. If your project already has history, before you approve it runs the zones over your last commits and tells you how many would have come out of each color. It asks only what changes a zone or a gate: money, accounts and permissions, personal data, what can't be undone.
  3. It looks at which agents you have installed and which models each one offers, and proposes which one does the light work, which the standard and which the deep, and which one reviews. You decide, and before it counts it checks that each one starts.
  4. It prepares the protections with you and tries them. It writes which files ask for your review, and what has to be applied on your platform you apply yourself, including your CI running the layer on every change. Then it tries four things from the environment the building agent will work in, pushing to the main branch among them. If any of them succeeds, your blueprint says so, and the agent not touching what judges it stays a rule.
  5. You approve the blueprint, or your team does if you work with one: with a commit or a pull request from a person, never from an agent, or with your yes in the conversation if you have no platform. It asks you in a short message: which files to read and, before you say yes, what doesn't protect anything yet or what it couldn't confirm.
  6. Another agent sets up the standing checks, once for the whole project, and a reviewer tries to fool them and checks that the blueprint asks for what each gate says. One that some colors ask for and others don't, such as mutation testing if you keep it for red, judges only what each change adds or modifies. And if one has to reach a number, it proposes starting from the one its tool gives or your code already reaches, not from the highest. A number nobody can reach doesn't make the gate stricter, it keeps it shut. No change starts until they are on the main branch.

Asking for a change

Ask in your own words, without naming any skill: in a project with a blueprint, gdd loads by itself.

Add a size filter to the catalog

Your agent gives the change the color of the most delicate thing it touches. If it touches something no zone covers, it judges it in that change, tells you why and asks you when it can't tell. If you ask for several things you could use separately, before it writes anything it proposes an order: one line per part, with what you could do with it and roughly how long it would take. Without your yes to the parts, it takes the whole request as one change.

Approving what has to pass

On green there's nothing to approve: there's no brief and no scenarios, and the change goes through the checks that already exist. On yellow and red, your agent first writes the rules of what changes, in its capability's file inside features/, and underneath them the scenarios that check them, the bad cases too. On red, and on yellow if the change alters another zone's data, a reviewer reads the brief before it reaches you and says what it leaves out.

Then, if your blueprint asks for your approval, as the template does on yellow and red, a message reaches you, under 150 words, or 200 on red, ending in one question a yes answers. It brings:

  • each new or changed rule, on its own line;
  • what your agent assumed on its own, which is marked @assumed in the file;
  • what can wait for another change, marked @question and with no scenarios;
  • how you'll know whether it helped, and where to look;
  • the technical decisions that concern you;
  • on red, also the rules of your product it touches and up to three things it leaves out.

Say yes or correct a rule. You can approve it in the conversation, with a commit of yours or, better on red, in a pull request from gdd-seal/<id>, which you approve and nobody merges: the brief reaches the main branch with the change. Your approval holds while what has to pass stays the same.

Until it's in

You do nothing here, unless something needs you. Three separate agents do the work, with the layer in between:

  1. The cases. Another agent, never the one that builds, writes one test per scenario, sees each one fail for the right reason and seals them on gdd-seal/<id>. On red it also sees each one pass and breaks it once on purpose.
  2. The build. The builder works on a branch of its own, with the brief and the seal. It never gets the log, or the conversation where its cases were made.
  3. The gates. The layer runs the checks on the change's exact commit, away from the builder's copy: in your CI or in a copy of its own, which on the rule degree is still within its reach, and your agent tells you so. If one fails, the change goes back with the command, its exit code and its last lines, and gets fixed in another round, while there are rounds left.
  4. The reviewer. An agent that has seen none of the above works out the color again from what was actually touched: it can only go up, and if it does, the change goes back to Brief. On green it checks the color and the seal. On yellow it reads the change against its brief and checks whether the sealed tests are tight enough. On red it also runs the change and tries to make it fail. Its verdict is pass, fix or stop. Each finding it asks to fix carries its citation and, on red, the command that reproduces it; otherwise the layer sends it back.

With everything passed, the change is brought up to date with your main branch and the checks run again. On red, with the template, you review the change at the end, in your platform's pull request, with the evidence the reviewer leaves you: screenshots, a short video or the output before and after. No agent approves anything.

When it's in, you get one line per gate, as in Get started, and each product choice the builder made on a line of its own, so you can correct it if it isn't yours.

When it needs you

While it works, it talks to you about your product and not about the method, and briefly: one line per gate, passed or failed, and the detail only when something fails, when you have to decide or when you ask for it. When it needs something from you, it asks one thing, with what it would do, so that a yes is enough.

If you tell it to decide, it decides how the work gets done and stops asking you about that. What stays yours, or your team's, is approving the blueprint and what has to pass, anything that loosens a check and what can't be undone.

So even if you've told it to decide, it won't record as debt a check that already fails without the change, or put it in quarantine, because that loosens it. Nor does it build anything while the check keeps failing, because no change would get through. It proposes one way out, something like this: "Your footer fix is written but not in yet, because a test of the cart's text already failed before I touched anything. I'd fix that first, on its own, and then both would go in. Is that all right?"

Closing: whether it helped

A change isn't closed when it's in, but when someone has looked at whether it helped (The five moments). The brief wrote it down before anyone built: what you expected to change for whoever uses it, what signal will tell and where to look. Closing never starts by itself, and a change in use doesn't hold up the next one.

.gdd/gdd next shows you the changes in use that wait for your verdict, with what was expected from each. When you want to look at one, tell your agent:

Did the size filter help?

Your agent goes to the place the brief named and gathers what can be seen, each fact with its source and its date, never from the builder's report or the change's own tests. If it can't reach that place, it tells you and you look. Then it puts it next to what you expected and asks you, without proposing an answer. The verdict is yours, or belongs to whoever your blueprint names, even if you've told it to decide:

VerdictWhat happens
YesThe change is closed.
Not yetIt stays in use and in sight, and your agent doesn't ask again until you come back to it.
NoIt's closed and nothing more gets built on it. If it works as written and didn't help, your agent proposes a new request on the list, or nothing if you accept it as it is. If it broke a rule or did harm, the fix is a normal change, and what slipped through goes to Harden.

What promised nothing to whoever uses your product, like a green change, closes by itself once the days in your blueprint's closing go by with nothing against it, 7 in the template.

Harden: one check more, or one less

Harden comes on two occasions: when something gets past every gate, or keeps repeating, and when a gate stops adding anything. Both weigh the same. It never starts by itself: a change leaves one proposal at most on the list, which waits for you to take it up between changes.

When something slips through. Fixing it is a normal change. Hardening is the other half: what it would have taken to catch it stays as a check. Tell your agent what slipped through and where you saw it, or what keeps repeating:

An expired discount code got through, I saw it in yesterday's orders
  1. Someone who knows that part of the product confirms the failure is real and that it was there.
  2. Your agent reads the brief and the log of the change that let it through, and works out which gate should have caught it and what it was missing.
  3. It proposes the scenario that would have caught it, with its bad case first, and the check that will cover it: a machine check where a machine can tell, a question for the reviewer where none can. Never one more paragraph of instructions, which costs at every gate and checks nothing.
  4. You approve the change to the blueprint and its gates, or your team does, with a commit or a pull request from a person.
  5. Another agent sets up the check, and a reviewer tries to fool it before it counts.

When a gate is too much. Tell it which one is in your way, and your agent reads in the logs what it has caught and what it has cost. If it no longer adds anything, it proposes relaxing or retiring it with those figures, and you or your team approve it. A new model is no reason to drop a check, because the check judges your product and not the model.

When it stops

Most of the time your agent goes on by itself and tells you in one line why it stopped. What each thing you might see means, and what you do, is in When it stops, symptom by symptom. Nothing gets fixed by loosening a check: when one looks wrong, only whoever wrote it or a person corrects it, never the agent that has to pass it.

What they leave in your repository

Everything ends up in files you can read, which make sense without the skills: the blueprint and its gates, the constitution, the brief of each change with its scenarios, and its log. Each one, with who writes it and what you do with it, is in What it leaves in your repository.

What they don't guarantee

What they don't give you is a guarantee. Without an engine, the agent not touching what judges it is a rule: the skills tell it so and then check that it hasn't, but nothing stops it. The layer keeps the order and the log from depending on the agent's memory, but it doesn't change that. With protected branches and the permissions of your platform you get a good part of the way, and your blueprint says which of the three degrees you're at, rule, platform or engine (How a gate gets fooled). Only an engine gives the full guarantee, and what's missing without one is in The engine.
