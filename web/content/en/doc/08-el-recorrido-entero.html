The whole path

The delicate case had one gate and your approval. My whole path, the one I use in my projects, has five gates and picks up with the same discount codes.

You don't need all of this to start: it's as far as you can take it, and what T-minus, the engine I'm building, will automate. The stumbles, in italics, are made up, so you can see what happens when something goes wrong. If a word doesn't ring a bell, it's in the Glossary.

With the skills. The skills already do a good part of this path: your agent writes the rules and the scenarios with you, another writes the cases, another the code, a reviewer checks it and the layer counts the caps and writes the log. The rest, such as deploying to staging before the last approval, you add if your project asks for it.

The five gates

They are the preparation, which a person approves at the start of the project, and four on every change:

GateWhenWho decides
PreparationOnce, at the start of the projectA person
ScenariosOn every change, before anyone buildsA person
LoopWhile the code is being writtenMachines
PRWhen the change reaches the pull requestMachines
ReleaseBefore the change is mergedA person

The arrows on the left of the drawing are the trips back when something fails. Underneath they are still the steps of a change, split up my way. After the release come Close, where I look at whether it helped, and Harden.

First, the preparation

At the start of the project, I create the blueprint, the agent's environment (everything around it: tools, permissions and checks) and the gates, and a person approves it. Preparation gate: person.

I add the skills for each technology I'm going to use (React's, Rust's...), on top of GDD's, the linters and the deterministic tools that will help me in the production process. When there are ready-made rules for my area, I'll start from them, because they'll bring almost all of that; none have been published yet (The kits).

In the store, I mark payments as a delicate zone and the agents work without access to production.

On every change

  1. I define what has to pass. I start by talking with the orchestrator: I tell it what I want and it helps me pin it down, push back on it or find a better solution. With that it writes the rules of what the change touches and the feature's scenarios in Gherkin, and I note what I expect from the change and where I'll look.

    I read them and approve or reject them, and once they're approved, a large share of the use cases can already be automated. If what I ask for is several things, it proposes parts before starting, and I decide. Scenarios gate: person.

    Here I reject the first version: an expired code is missing.

  2. I brief the cases. An agent writes the tests for those features, e2e ones too (the ones that run through the whole application, the way a user would). CI checks that they fail, and for the right reason. A reviewer checks that they cover the scenarios I approved and tries a wrong solution on them on purpose.

    If return 45 opened the gate, the tests would go back to whoever wrote them.

  3. I brief another agent on the code. A different agent writes the code that makes those tests pass and runs it through several linters, which warn it about quality and security problems. It repeats that loop until a reviewer with a clean context runs the change and only passes it if it works. Loop gate: machines.

    With the skills, the reviewer reads the change on yellow and, on red, as here, runs it as well. Its word doesn't count either: each finding of a fix carries its citation and, on red, the command that reproduces it, and on red its pass says what it ran.

    Here comes the first rejection: the discount is duplicated in the cart and in the checkout.

  4. If the agent thinks a test is wrong, it tells me. It doesn't touch it: it stops and says so. The check gets fixed by whoever made it or by a person, never by whoever is getting through the gate.

    Here, three items at €9.95 with 10% off come to €26.87 on the total and €26.88 when rounding per line. The test expects the first and the cart does the second. It isn't an agent error: it's a decision nobody had made, and I make it.

  5. If it gets stuck, it stops. In my projects, that loop can only repeat twice. On the third, the orchestrator decides whether the scope or the approach has to change, and talks it over with me.

    With the skills, each color's caps are in your blueprint: the template gives two fix rounds per gate and three per change, on yellow and on red. When they run out, your agent tells you what works, what's missing and what another attempt would buy.

  6. I let it reach the PR, but I don't merge anything yet. When the reviewer passes it, the agent pushes the PR. The e2e tests run there and another reviewer looks at the whole thing, and if something has to change, the branch goes back to the agent. PR gate: machines.

    Here an e2e test fails: the order summary still shows the price without the discount.

  7. I look where it matters. If all goes well and the change touches red zones, as it does here, I review the code and check how it would be undone.

  8. I give the last approval. The change is deployed from its branch to staging, or behind a feature flag (the feature stays off until someone turns it on), depending on the project. If it touches red zones, I wait until I've reviewed it; if not, it deploys on its own. There I check that it works and approve. Release gate: person.

    Only then does CI check everything again and, if it passes, the change is merged and ships. If I say no, I close the PR, and there's nothing to undo on the main branch.

    GDD doesn't require this approval: it's a person's check I've chosen in my projects, and for anything delicate I recommend it. With the skills, where your blueprint asks you to approve the change, it reaches you with the evidence the reviewer leaves: screenshots, a short video or the output before and after.

  9. I look at whether it helped. The change is already in use, but for me it isn't closed yet. When I come back to it, the orchestrator shows me what can be seen, taken from the orders and not from its own report, and I compare it with what I noted at the start.

    I say yes, not yet or no. Until I look, the change waits in plain sight, with how long it has been there, and it doesn't hold up what comes next.

    Here I say not yet: in two weeks only three orders have used a code, and I want to see a whole campaign.

  10. I harden what failed. With the change already out, the orchestrator analyzes what failed along the way and the cracks it saw, and proposes what to harden.

    Here it proposes an architecture rule: the discount is only calculated in one place.

    If a negative discount had slipped through, that case would become a rule, with its test, and a question on the reviewer's exam. Hardening goes the other way too: if a check hasn't caught anything in a long while and only costs, it proposes relaxing or retiring it, from the same logs.

    And if what happened touches the agent's environment, I go over the blueprint and propose the tweak, and a person approves it: an agent never decides that.

When the path goes sideways

If this happensWhat happens
The change is the text on a buttonIt's green: the same gates, at a different difficulty. The standing checks pass and I, to start with, don't review the code.
There is an emergency in productionThe first thing I try is to undo it. If a fix is needed, a person approves it through the emergency gate, it gets recorded, and afterward it goes through the normal gates.
Almost everything comes out redThe blueprint is wrong: you've marked too much as delicate. Preparing warns you, since it runs your zones over your last commits before you approve the blueprint, and later .gdd/gdd metrics tells you how many changes come out of each color.
When approving, you ask for a tweakIt goes back to the agent with your words. Since it changes what you saw, the gates and your approval are requested again.
Nobody looks at whether it helpedIf it promised something, it stays in use and not closed, in plain sight, with how long it has been there: it doesn't close by itself, it isn't canceled and it doesn't hold up what comes next. What promised nothing, like a typo, closes by itself once the days your blueprint sets go by without it doing harm.
A rule can't be decided yetIt stays as a question, tagged @question, in sight and with no scenarios, until a later change.
There's a question to answer before buildingI treat it as a spike, an exploration with fewer gates: its code stays on its branch and only the decision comes through.

So far I've assumed the agent plays fair. What happens when it doesn't is in How a gate gets fooled.

On this path I give the last approval before merging. Does GDD require it on every change?

  • No: it's a person's check I chose, and where a person approves is up to your blueprint.
  • Yes: it's the release gate, and without it no change can be merged.

That's it. It's my way of working, not a rule of the method. The template proposes reviewing the change at the end only on red, and what each color asks for is for you, or your team, to decide in the blueprint.
