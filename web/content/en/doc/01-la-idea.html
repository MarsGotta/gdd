The idea

You ask your agent for something, it works for a good while and at the end it tells you "done." You try it, and it isn't. So you ask again, more carefully, you write longer specifications, and it says "done" again with the same confidence. You end up reviewing everything yourself, which was exactly what you wanted to avoid.

I spent a long time like that, until I stopped trying to convince it and started putting gates on it. That's where Gate-Driven Development (GDD) comes from, and I tell the longer story in the Article.

What a gate is

A gate is what the agent has to prove before moving on, and you decide it before it starts: that a customer gets charged only once, that the total never drops below zero. Its word opens nothing.

Check
What opens the gate. It's something you probably already use and the agent can't touch, like a test, a linter or another person's review. You get through a gate only if all of its checks pass.
Context
What you give the agent so it can get through the gate. It helps it get through, but it proves nothing.
Risk
How much damage the change can do to your product. It decides how much the agent is asked for and whether it calls you.
Harden
What you do when something slips through, which it will. You don't stop at fixing it: whatever would have been needed to catch it stays as one more check, and it can't get in that way again. And a check that no longer catches anything, you relax or retire.

If the change is a typo, you barely notice. If it touches payment, you ask for a lot more, and it may call you to look at it yourself before going on. The rest of the time it works alone.

An escape room

Picture an apartment full of doors. At the entrance, a receptionist welcomes you to the most fun escape room you will ever play: the one about building a product. Each door hides a puzzle, and every room has clues to solve it. If you solve it, they hand you a key and you move on to the next one, until the mission is complete.

The locks change with the difficulty, even when the doors are the same. Some doors are watched by a robot that checks you aren't cheating. Others, by a person you show what you have done, who decides whether to let you through.

At the end, if someone managed to cheat (very bad, right?) or the game was too easy, the receptionist reviews what happened and, if needed, hardens the gates.

Now switch sides. You are no longer the one entering the escape room: you are the one building it. The locks of the game are your checks, the clues are the context and the players are your agents. The robot watching the doors is another agent, which I call the reviewer.

Risk is the difficulty

In your project, difficulty is risk: how much damage can this change do to my product? I think of it as a traffic light, because that color also decides how much is left for you to do.

RiskFor exampleHow the gates are setWhat you have to do
Green (low)Changing some textMachines handle it, and some gate can stay open.Nothing: you ask for the change and, if it passes the gates, it ships.
Yellow (medium)A new filter in a listOn top of the machines, a reviewer usually looks at it, and a person if you decide so.Approve its rules before anyone builds.
Red (high)Something that touches paymentSome gates stop until you give your approval.Approve its rules, and review the change at the end.

That table is what I would do, and what the blueprint template brings to start with: the blueprint is the document where you say what is delicate and what each color asks for. What you approve in each one is up to you, or your team: in some projects a green change ships without anyone looking at it, and in others even green waits for an approval. What holds in all of them is that more risk asks for more.

What you design

That is the basis of GDD: we don't develop code, or even tests. We write what must be true and design the gates our agents go through.

What you get is a quality product without supervising every step. What you used to look at yourself is now looked at by the gates, and you're left with the decisions that need someone.

And the specifications? They don't disappear, but they stay at what can be executed. A long specification is tedious to read, and reading it doesn't ensure the agent will follow it. That's why what must be true gets written as short rules and scenarios.
