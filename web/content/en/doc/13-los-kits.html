The kits

Imagine you're starting a project from scratch and you have to decide every rule yourself: what gets checked, with which tools, what instructions each agent receives. That's what kits are for: ready-made rules for an area, which you start from and then adjust. They are part of the engine, but they are not the engine: the engine is the same for every area, and what changes is the kit it loads.

What a kit brings

It brings its configuration, its standard checks, the tools you need to download and the instructions for the agents, like skills and good practices. The one T-minus will bring is the software kit. Your blueprint is that kit plus your team's decisions, and each change then adds its own custom check.

A kit also decides how the method is configured, for example how much is demanded at each color of the risk table. Choosing a kit changes your blueprint, and the team decides that (The core).

If you look inside the engine, you'll find three more words: sealing a check means putting it out of the agent's reach, the box is the bounded environment where the agent works, and the record keeps who did what, which gate judged it and what it cost.

When a check is missing

If your area needs a check that doesn't exist, you build it outside the engine and plug it in like any other. In design, for example, a gate can compare each component, pixel by pixel, with its reference from the design system and its tokens. To the engine it's the same as Cucumber or a linter: a command that says whether it passes or not. And like any check, the agent can't touch it, neither the script nor the references.

With a kit you have somewhere to start. To choose which checks to put at each stop, see the Good practices.
