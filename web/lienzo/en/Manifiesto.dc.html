Your agent says "done" and the gate checks if it's true.

All of GDD fits in that rule, seven principles and five moments.

Manifesto v0.11, draft for reviewNovember 18, 2026Marcela Gotta

The seven principles

  1. 1

    Gates come first, and nobody skips them, not even you.

    They are defined before the work, so you don't check what the agent did instead of what was needed. Emergencies have a gate of their own, decided beforehand: a person opens it, it's written down, and the change goes through the others afterwards.

  2. 2

    A gate that never closes is not a gate.

    Before you trust it, check that it closes, and that it closes for the right reason.

  3. 3

    To each gate, its context.

    Without context, the agent guesses and fails. With too much, it gets lost and costs more. Context helps it get through the gate, but it proves nothing.

  4. 4

    You get through a gate with facts, not words.

    Saying "done" opens nothing. Without facts, the agent stops and asks.

  5. 5

    Whoever goes through the gate doesn't decide how it's validated.

    The agent never touches the tests that judge it, just as a student doesn't grade their own exam. If a check is wrong, whoever wrote it or a person corrects it.

  6. 6

    More risk, more checks, and your approval only where it matters.

    Risk decides, not the size of the change. More risk isn't always more tests: sometimes it means a reviewer or a person. Approving everything leads to approving without reading.

  7. 7

    Gates with cracks get hardened.

    What slips through or keeps repeating becomes a check. A gate that no longer catches anything, or that costs more than it protects, is relaxed or retired, with the same evidence you would ask for to add it.

The five moments

MomentWhenWhat happens
PrepareAt the start, and when the blueprint falls short.The blueprint is written, the gates are built and someone tries to fool them on purpose. No change starts until they hold.
BriefIn each change, before it starts.Rules, a few scenarios and a first estimate of the risk. The tests are written by whoever won't build.
ExecuteIn each change.The agent goes through the gates one by one. If it gets stuck, it stops and asks. The risk is worked out again from what was touched, and it can only go up.
CloseWith the change already in use.A person checks whether it helped: yes, not yet or no. A "no" also closes it, and opens a new brief.
HardenWhen something slips through or keeps repeating.Cracks become checks, and what no longer adds anything is relaxed or retired. The blueprint changes, you go back to Prepare and a person approves it.

Risk

Risk measures how much harm a change can do to your product. What each level asks for is decided by you or your team, and it is written in the blueprint.

GreenChanging a text. Green is only what the blueprint marks as green.
YellowA new filter on a list. What isn't classified is analyzed, and if it isn't clear, someone asks.
RedSomething that touches payment. Nothing ships without a tested way to undo it, and if there isn't one, a person decides knowing that.

What can't be undone is never green.

When a gate holds

  • It was decided before the agent started.
  • It is checked without trusting what the agent says.
  • It is out of the agent's reach. Checking from the same machine it works on doesn't count.
  • It has been seen to reject something done wrong, real or planted on purpose.

Who answers for it

Every change has an author who answers for it, the person in charge. The AI never answers for you.

The manifesto is the reference. If T-minus, another engine or the documentation contradicts it, the manifesto wins.
