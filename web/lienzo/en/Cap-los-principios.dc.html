The seven principles

What gives the gates their meaning.

Before you start, stop 4 of 5

1. Gates come first, and nobody skips them, not even you

If you define the check after you see the result, you check what the agent did, not what you needed. Emergencies have a gate of their own, decided in advance.

2. A gate that never closes is not a gate

A new check has to fail for the right reason and catch wrong solutions. A test that also passes with return 40 is hollow.

3. To each gate, its context

Give each gate only what it needs. More context proves nothing, and what goes along on every call has to be short: a context file for the whole repository raises the cost without helping.

4. You get through a gate with facts, not words

Saying "done" isn't enough. An agent that gets stuck stops and asks, and its attempts, its time and its cost have a cap.

5. Whoever goes through the gate doesn't decide how it's validated

The agent doesn't edit the tests that judge it. If it thinks a check is wrong, it stops and says so. Whoever wrote the check or a person fixes it.

6. More risk, more checks, and your approval only where it matters

Risk decides, not the size of the change. A person reviews only where the blueprint asks for it.

7. Gates with cracks get hardened

Everything that slips through, whether an incident or a rollback, becomes a check. A gate that no longer catches anything is relaxed or retired, with evidence.

Before you cross: A new test also passes with return 40. Is it a gate?

That's not it. It's hollow. A gate has to close, and for the right reason.
